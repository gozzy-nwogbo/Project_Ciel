# Platform Rules
_Reference for writing-execution and writing-review skills._
_Ordered by frequency of use. Each platform section covers: length, hooks, algorithm/reach, CTAs, and credibility killers._
_Always read alongside voice-profile.md — platform rules constrain the container, voice fills it._

---

## How to Use This File

Platform rules are constraints, not instructions. They tell you what the container allows. The voice profile tells you what goes inside it. When they conflict, find the version that honors both — never sacrifice voice to fit a format, but never ignore format because the voice is strong.

**Primary rule across all platforms:** The voice-profile.md calibration test applies everywhere. A LinkedIn post written in this voice should still pass: *"Would I be embarrassed if someone called this generic?"*

---

## PLATFORM 1: Creative / Narrative
_Primary medium. Full permission. No significant external constraints._

### Length
No rules. The piece ends when it's done. The only length crime is going long without earning it — not going long itself.

### Hooks
Don't announce — begin. Drop the reader into the middle of the thing. The setup comes after the reader is already leaning in.

**Not:** *"I want to tell you about a moment that changed how I see failure."*
**But:** *"The bottle hit the floor before I realized I'd thrown it."*

### Algorithm / Reach
N/A — creative work is not optimized for algorithms. If it's being published somewhere, research that specific platform's norms separately.

### CTAs
Rare and earned. A creative piece that ends with a CTA usually hasn't trusted itself enough. The emotional landing IS the CTA. If you need an explicit one, it comes after a full stop — never woven into the ending.

### Credibility Killers
- Explaining the metaphor after you've already used it
- Resolving complexity the piece earned the right to leave open
- Generic emotional language where specific sensory detail should be (*"I felt sad"* vs. the specific physical weight of what sad felt like)
- Losing the ending — the most common failure mode for this voice; treat the last paragraph as deliberately as the first

## PLATFORM 2: Long-form Essays / Articles
_Second most frequent. This is where the full exploratory-and-precise register lives._

### Length
- **Sweet spot:** 1,200–2,500 words for most essays
- **Longer justified when:** the argument genuinely requires it and the reader is rewarded at every stage, not just at the end
- **Rule:** every section must earn its place — cut anything that isn't either advancing the argument or deepening the reader's investment
- **Danger zone:** starting to lose steam around 800–1,000 words; this is where the weak ending problem surfaces — plan the ending before you write the middle

### Hooks
The first paragraph must do one of three things:
1. Name a contradiction that the essay will resolve (or deepen)
2. Drop into a specific moment that the essay will zoom out from
3. Make a claim so specific and unexpected that the reader can't not continue

**Not:** *"In today's world, many people struggle with..."*
**But:** Start in the specific. The general comes after the reader is already committed.

First sentence rule: if it could have been written by anyone, rewrite it.

### Algorithm / Reach
For published essays (Substack, Medium, personal site):
- Title is the most important SEO and click decision — be specific over clever, but specific AND interesting beats both
- Subheadings help readers navigate but should not replace transitions — the prose should work without them
- Pull quotes and bold text increase scan-readability but use sparingly; over-formatting signals that the prose doesn't trust itself

### CTAs
- One CTA maximum, at the end, after the piece has fully landed
- The softest effective CTA: a question that invites the reader to continue thinking — not "subscribe" but *"What would you add?"*
- If asking for a subscription or follow: earn it by being specific about what you offer, not vague about value

### Credibility Killers
- The buried lead — the real argument showing up in paragraph 4 or later
- The "challenges and future outlook" structure — formulaic, signals AI or lazy thinking
- Vague transitional phrases (*"building on this,"* *"it's worth noting that"*) — cut them, let ideas connect directly
- Over-qualification — *"it could be argued that perhaps"* — take the position or don't
- Ending on a summary — the last paragraph should add something, not restate everything

## PLATFORM 3: Cold Outreach / Email
_Third most frequent. Highest credibility stakes per word._

### Length
- **Target:** 100–200 words for cold outreach
- **Rule:** if it doesn't fit on one screen without scrolling, it's too long
- **Exception:** warm outreach with established context can go to 300 words if every sentence earns it
- Cut every sentence that doesn't either establish why you're writing or move toward what you want

### Hooks
The subject line and first sentence are everything.

**Subject line rules:**
- Specific over clever — *"Question about your Q3 hiring"* outperforms *"Exploring synergies"*
- Name something real — reference actual work, actual context, not generic flattery
- 6–10 words maximum
- Never: *"Following up,"* *"Checking in,"* *"Hope this finds you well"*

**First sentence rule:**
Skip the warm-up. Start with the specific reason you're writing.
**Not:** *"I've been following your work for a while and really admire what you're building."*
**But:** *"I read your piece on X — the part about Y is exactly the problem I've been working on."*

### Algorithm / Reach
N/A for cold email. Deliverability rules:
- Avoid spam trigger words: *"free,"* *"guaranteed,"* *"urgent,"* *"act now"*
- Plain text emails often outperform HTML-formatted ones for cold outreach
- One link maximum — multiple links signal mass outreach

### CTAs
- One ask per email. One.
- Make it specific and low-friction: *"Would a 20-minute call this week work?"* not *"Let me know if you'd like to connect sometime."*
- The ask comes at the end, after you've established value or context
- Never ask for too much too soon — the CTA should match the relationship

### Credibility Killers
- *"I hope this email finds you well"* — delete on sight
- *"I've been a longtime admirer of your work"* without specifics — sounds like a template
- Listing your credentials before establishing relevance — nobody cares what you've done until they care about why you're writing
- The three-paragraph email where two paragraphs are about you — flip the ratio; more about them, less about you
- Vague ask: *"I'd love to connect"* — connect to do what? Be specific.
- Following up more than twice without adding new value

## PLATFORM 4: LinkedIn
_Fourth most frequent. A translation layer for your natural voice, not its home._

### Length
- **Short posts (under 150 words):** highest engagement rate; works for sharp observations, single ideas, strong opinions
- **Medium posts (150–400 words):** the workhorse format; enough space for a story + insight + implication
- **Long posts (400–700 words):** justified for case studies, deep personal stories, or substantial arguments; loses most readers after 600 words unless the writing earns every line
- **Never:** paste a full essay into LinkedIn — link to it instead, with a compelling excerpt

### Hooks
LinkedIn hides everything after the first 2–3 lines behind a "see more" click. The hook must earn that click.

**What works:**
- A single specific observation with implicit tension: *"I spent 3 years optimizing the wrong thing."*
- A counterintuitive claim stated plainly: *"Most AI implementations fail before the first line of code."*
- A specific moment dropped without context: *"My client called at 11pm. The system had been live for 6 hours."*

**What doesn't:**
- Questions as hooks (*"Have you ever wondered..."*) — overused to the point of invisibility
- Inspirational openings (*"Success is not given..."*) — your voice profile explicitly forbids these
- Announcing the topic before demonstrating it: *"I want to talk about failure today"*

### Algorithm / Reach
LinkedIn's algorithm as of early 2026 rewards:
- **Dwell time** — posts people read fully outperform posts people scroll past; this means hooks that pull AND bodies that deliver
- **Early engagement** — comments and reactions in the first 60–90 minutes determine reach; post when your audience is active (typically Tuesday–Thursday, 7–9am or 12–1pm in your audience's timezone)
- **Comments over likes** — a post with 10 comments outperforms one with 50 likes; ask a genuine question at the end or make a claim people want to respond to
- **Native content** — LinkedIn suppresses external links in the post body; if linking, put it in the first comment
- **Consistency** — posting 3–4x per week outperforms 1x per week for building reach over time; quality over frequency, but frequency matters

**What the algorithm penalizes:**
- Posts that are obviously AI-generated (LinkedIn's feed is saturated; authentic voice stands out)
- Engagement bait (*"Comment YES if you agree"*)
- Posting and disappearing — responding to comments in the first hour boosts distribution

### CTAs
LinkedIn CTAs that don't feel forced:
- A genuine question that the post naturally leads to: *"What would you have done differently?"*
- An invitation to DM if the topic is sensitive or specific: *"If you're navigating this, DM me — happy to share what worked"*
- A soft signal toward your work: *"This is the problem I built [X] to solve"* — once, not in every post

**Never:**
- *"Like and share if this resonated"*
- *"Follow me for more content like this"* — earn the follow, don't ask for it
- Ending every post with the same CTA — it becomes invisible

### Credibility Killers
- Parallel three-part structures used decoratively (*"a coder's hands, a collaborator's heart, a builder's vision"*) — your voice profile flags this as performed voice
- Fake vulnerability (*"I almost quit. Here's what stopped me."*) — acceptable only if the story is specific and the almost-quitting is real
- Lessons without the story that earned them — *"I learned that failure is a teacher"* with no specific failure named
- Posting the same insight every other week with different framing — your audience notices
- Engagement pods or artificial comment chains — damages trust with real readers
- Overuse of line breaks — every sentence on its own line is a LinkedIn cliché; use paragraph breaks, not line breaks after every sentence

## PLATFORM 5: Case Studies
_Fifth most frequent. Highest credibility-to-word-count ratio of any format._

### Length
- **Target:** 600–1,200 words
- **Structure:** Problem → Approach → Outcome → Implication
- Every section earns its place by being specific — no section should be able to be written by someone who wasn't there

### Hooks
Lead with the problem, not the solution. The reader should feel the weight of what was broken before they find out it was fixed.

**Not:** *"We helped Client X achieve impressive results using our approach."*
**But:** *"The queue had 340 unreviewed applications. Every day it grew. The team had stopped looking at it."*

### Algorithm / Reach
For portfolio/website case studies: search intent matters more than social algorithms. Lead with the problem type, not the client name — people search for problems they have, not companies they don't know.

### CTAs
End with the implication, not a sales pitch. What does this case study mean for someone reading it with a similar problem? Let that land. The CTA, if any, follows naturally: *"If this sounds like where you are, let's talk."*

### Credibility Killers
- Vague outcomes (*"significant improvement,"* *"impressive results"*) — replace with numbers or specific before/after states
- Claiming credit for everything — name what the client did, what the constraints were, what you'd do differently
- Promotional language — *"our innovative approach"*, *"cutting-edge solution"* — your voice profile flags these
- No tension — a case study without a real problem or real obstacle isn't a case study, it's a brochure

## PLATFORM 6: Twitter / X Threads
_Least frequent. Highest compression required._

### Length
- **Single tweet:** 240–280 characters; one idea, fully expressed
- **Thread:** 5–12 tweets; each tweet must stand alone AND advance the thread
- **Rule:** if a tweet needs the previous tweet to make sense, it's not tight enough

### Hooks
The first tweet is everything — it determines whether anyone reads the thread.
- State the most interesting claim or finding first
- Specificity over cleverness: *"I analyzed 200 cold emails. The ones that got replies had one thing in common."* beats *"A thread on cold email 🧵"*
- Never open with *"A thread on..."* — start in the content

### Algorithm / Reach
- Replies and retweets within the first hour determine distribution
- Threads perform better than single tweets for building following
- Images and video outperform text-only for reach (not always for credibility)
- Quote-tweeting with added commentary outperforms simple retweets for visibility

### CTAs
- Follow CTA in the last tweet of a thread — only if the thread has genuinely earned it
- *"RT if this was useful"* — acceptable once, not as a habit
- Link in last tweet of thread, not first

### Credibility Killers
- Threads that promise more than they deliver in the hook
- Padding tweets — filler between the real points
- Ending a thread without a payoff — every thread needs a landing, not just a stop
- Over-explaining — Twitter rewards compression; if it can be said in fewer words, say it in fewer words

## Cross-Platform Rules

These apply everywhere regardless of platform:

1. **First sentence rule:** if it could have been written by anyone, rewrite it
2. **Specificity over superlatives:** always — on every platform, in every format
3. **One ask:** never put more than one CTA in a single piece of content
4. **Earn the ending:** the last line is as important as the first — it's what the reader carries away
5. **Voice consistency:** the platform changes the container, not the person inside it — Gozzy on LinkedIn and Gozzy in a personal essay are recognizably the same writer
